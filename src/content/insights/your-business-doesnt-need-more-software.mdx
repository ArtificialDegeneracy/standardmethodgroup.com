Running a small business has never involved more technology.

There's software for scheduling, estimating, invoicing, customer management, marketing, communication, and just about everything else. And now a new wave of tools promises to handle nearly all of it for you.

Here's what I'd ask first: what if the problem isn't the technology you're using? What if it's how the work gets done?

## The problem with adding another tool

Picture a local service business that gets 20 new customer inquiries every week. Some come through the website. Others arrive by phone, email, or text.

The owner is busy managing jobs, employees, and customers. Sometimes inquiries get answered right away. Other times they sit until someone remembers to follow up.

Eventually the business decides it needs a customer relationship management (CRM) system. That's a reasonable idea. But buying the software doesn't solve the problem on its own.

Someone still has to decide who responds to inquiries, how quickly they should respond, where customer information gets recorded, and what happens when a potential customer doesn't answer.

Without those decisions, the business has just bought a more expensive place to lose track of customers.

## Start with the process, not the platform

Before investing in another system, ask three questions.

<Steps>
<Step question="Where is work getting stuck?">

Look for the moments when someone is waiting on information, repeating a task, or wondering what happens next.

Maybe estimates aren't being followed up on. Maybe scheduling takes too many phone calls. Maybe the owner is the only person who knows the status of every job.

These are often process problems before they're technology problems.

</Step>
<Step question="What should happen instead?">

Define what a successful process looks like. For example, every new inquiry might need to be acknowledged, assigned to someone, and tracked until it's resolved.

That's a business process. It doesn't take expensive software to define it.

</Step>
<Step question="Can technology make that process easier?">

Once the process is clear, technology becomes much more useful.

- A simple routing rule might send new inquiries straight to the right employee.
- A [better website](/services/websites/) might make it easier for customers to request estimates.
- Connecting the systems you already use might eliminate duplicate data entry and unnecessary manual work.

Or the solution might be as simple as changing how the team communicates.

The right answer depends on the business.

</Step>
</Steps>

## New tools don't fix a broken process

When someone asks me about automation, I give the same answer every time:

> If we have a shit process and we automate it, we just do shit faster.

It's blunt, but it's the whole argument. If your team is entering the same information into three different systems, software that fills in those entries might save some time. But why does the information need to be entered three times in the first place?

Sometimes the better fix is getting rid of the work entirely. That's why the process comes before the technology.

## Small improvements can make a real difference

You don't have to overhaul the whole business. Take a company that spends 15 minutes preparing information by hand for every new job. Here's an example with made-up numbers:

At 100 jobs a month, that's 25 hours spent on one repetitive task. Cut it to five minutes per job and you get back about 17 hours a month.

That's time your people could spend with customers and on the jobs themselves.

And fixes like this are often simple.

## The goal isn't more technology. It's a better business.

Technology should make your business easier to run.

It should help employees do their jobs, make it easier for customers to work with you, and give owners a clearer view of what's happening. If it doesn't do those things, question whether it's the right investment.
