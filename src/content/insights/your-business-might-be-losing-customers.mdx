Say someone in Clearwater needs a contractor. They search Google, find three businesses that look good, and contact all three. The first replies within an hour. The second replies the next afternoon. The third never replies.

The first business won't win every time. Price, reputation, and availability still count. But it answered while the customer was still ready to talk, and the other two may never get that chance. They also may never find out they missed it.

## Most of the effort goes into getting the inquiry

Small businesses spend real time and money on websites, Google ads, reviews, and referrals. All of it exists to get a customer to reach out.

Then the inquiry lands somewhere. A website form goes to an inbox nobody checks. A voicemail sits under ten others. Someone answers a customer and forgets to book the next conversation. An estimate goes out and nobody follows up.

In each case the inquiry arrived and nobody owned it. More advertising or a new system wouldn't have fixed that.

A working inquiry process can still give the person answering it better information.

## Make the first conversation easier

We recently rebuilt the website for [Hammer Down Sportfishing](https://hammerdownsportfishing.com/), a Tampa Bay charter fishing business run by Captain Bryce Artze. His Wix site already sent customer inquiries to his email, so we kept that process.

We made the site easier to find through search, added clearer trip information and FAQs, and changed the form to ask about the trip someone wants and their preferred dates. Bryce spends much of his time on the water. When he checks an inquiry, he has more of what he needs to discuss the trip with that customer.

That's a useful question for any business: when an inquiry reaches the right person, does it give them enough to take the next step?

## Follow your last five inquiries

To see what works in your own process and where it could improve, think about the last five people who contacted your business, whether or not they ended up buying. For each one, answer these.

<Steps>
<Step question="Where did it come from?">

A call, a form, an email, a text, a social media message, a referral. If you'd have to check several systems to find all five, that's the first thing to fix.

</Step>
<Step question="Who was supposed to respond?">

Was one person assigned, or did everyone assume someone else had it?

</Step>
<Step question="How long did the customer wait?">

Did anyone reply while the customer was still looking, or did it depend on when someone happened to check messages?

</Step>
<Step question="Where could someone see its status?">

Could a second employee tell whether the customer was waiting on an estimate, a schedule, or a callback?

</Step>
<Step question="What happened in the end?">

Did it become a job, get declined, or just stop?

</Step>
</Steps>

If you can't answer these with confidence, you've found a place to improve. You didn't need to buy anything to find it.

## What a missed follow-up can cost

For a separate, hypothetical example, take a service business that gets 60 inquiries a month. Ten of them never get a timely reply. If three of those ten would have bought a $1,500 job, that's $4,500 a month.

## Set the rules before you buy a tool

In the first Operations Notes, [Your business doesn't need more software](/insights/your-business-doesnt-need-more-software/), I explained why it's worth understanding a process before adding another tool. Customer inquiries are a good example of where that matters. A CRM, an automated texting tool, or an AI assistant might help eventually. Before you pay for one, settle four things:

- You know where inquiries from each channel arrive.
- One person is responsible for replying.
- You've set how fast a reply should happen.
- You can tell whether the customer got what they needed.

Once those are settled, choosing a tool gets easier. It might be a form that routes requests to the right employee, a reminder in a system you already use, or a shared list the team checks each morning. Pick whichever one your team will actually use.

## Try this quick test

Open your own website as a customer would. Find every way to reach you: a contact form, an email address, a phone number, an appointment-booking link. Submit a test form request and check where it arrives. Open the booking link and make sure it works. Call the listed number and make sure it reaches the right person or voicemail.

Last, ask who owns the next step. Once a customer reaches out, who sees it, who replies, and how would you know if nobody did?

Everything may work. If it doesn't, you'll find the gap before a customer has gone elsewhere.
